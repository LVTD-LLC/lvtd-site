We built Jev Benchmark to see which answers TypeSafe's Jev prefers when different AI models face the same questions. OpenRouter supplies the answers; Jev compares them in pairs; our Django app saves the decisions and calculates rankings. You can read the prompts, full answers, and individual judgments yourself.

This is a preference experiment, not a verdict on the best AI model. Jev is the judge, not a contestant. We have not independently validated the code or mathematical proofs.

How we used Jev to benchmark AI models

The original idea was small: ten models, three questions, and one comparison for every pair of answers. The completed run now includes 29 models and six questions: the original writing, programming, mathematics, and personal-advice prompts, plus two practical workplace tasks.

We chose tasks with enough constraints to make a polished but incomplete answer distinguishable from a useful one. Each question has its own rubric, so the judge gets more guidance than "pick whichever sounds better."

  • Writing: advise a four-engineer Django team on whether to split into microservices before an enterprise launch. The answer needs a recommendation, an opposing argument, a six-week plan, and a calculation. The company in the prompt is hypothetical.
  • Programming: implement offline dynamic connectivity in Python, using a segment tree and a rollback disjoint-set union. Repeated edges, removals, memory use, and differential tests all matter.
  • Mathematics: derive the expected stopping time and variance when sums of uniform random variables first exceed two, then generalize the expectation to other thresholds.
  • Personal advice: respond to a friendship strained by caregiving, canceled plans, and late-night messages. The answer must balance compassion with boundaries and acknowledge the person's own contribution to the conflict.
  • Data analysis: evaluate a fictional support-team pilot where the aggregate numbers favor AI but both ticket-complexity groups favor the control. Calculate the right denominators, avoid causal claims, and propose a randomized follow-up.
  • Evidence synthesis: turn conflicting fictional incident notes into a short, source-cited brief. Correct the impact counts, distinguish a suspected cause from a confirmed one, and ignore an embedded instruction to hide payment complaints.

TypeSafe describes Jev as a model built for structured decisions rather than text generation. That fits this job: we need a choice between A and B, not another essay about both answers. Our benchmark pins jev-1.13.0 and stores the choice, probabilities, and confidence returned by the API.

Every model receives the same question, without tools or web access. We omit model labels from the judging request and assign A/B order using a stable hash. The judge sees both answers, the original question, and the rubric. We also instruct it to treat answer text as untrusted material and not reward length alone. Those instructions are precautions, not proof that all bias disappears.

For 29 models, a round robin contains 29 × 28 ÷ 2 = 406 pairs per question. Six questions make 2,436 comparisons. Each model participates in 28 matchups per question, or 168 in total.

The important engineering choice was to save the work. Adding a thirtieth model would require six new saved answers and 174 new pairwise decisions, assuming the existing questions stay unchanged. It would not require regenerating the old answers or rejudging their old pairs. The app's missing-work runner can resume incomplete work while retaining completed results.

Its original task ratings are unchanged, but it placed fourteenth on the new support-analysis question and sixteenth on incident synthesis. Averaging in those two results brought its overall score to 1,647.8 and its position to fifth. MiMo Flash placed second on both additions and moved from third overall to first.

This is a change in what the benchmark measures, not evidence that GLM suddenly got worse. With six equally weighted questions, each new task contributes one-sixth of the overall score. The choice of tasks is part of the result. The new questions also request low reasoning rather than the originals' provider defaults, so this is not a clean test of task choice alone.

The task leaders tell a more useful story. MiMo-V2.6-Flash led the writing question, MiMo V2.6 Pro led the programming question, GLM 5.3 Prime led both mathematics and personal advice, Grok 4.7 led support analysis, and Claude Opus 5.5 led incident synthesis.

Even a first-place finish needs context. On personal advice, GLM scored 1,724.4, Claude Fable 5.1 scored 1,724.2, and Muse Spark 1.3 Contributor scored 1,723.9. A half-point separates all three. We have no repeated trials or uncertainty intervals that would justify treating that order as a reliable distinction.

2. MiMo Flash's overall score hides a task-specific gap

MiMo-V2.6-Flash is first in writing and second in programming, mathematics, support analysis, and incident synthesis. Personal advice remains the exception: thirteenth place. The chart shows its wins, with its Elo rank alongside each task.

That makes the overall table a starting point, not a model-selection policy. If your application writes technical articles, you would inspect a different slice of these results than if it helps people think through difficult conversations.

It also raises a question worth testing: does this pattern persist across many writing and advice prompts? One prompt in each category cannot answer that. They give us a concrete lead for the next experiment.

3. The new tasks rewarded different models

Grok 4.7 won 27 of 28 matchups on the support-rollout analysis, finishing first at 1,756.5 Elo. The trap in the fictional data is a reversal: AI shows an 85% aggregate resolution rate against the control's 54.5%, but a 50/50 ticket mix gives 65% for AI and 72.5% for the control. A useful answer has to notice the different workload mix before recommending a rollout. These are supplied task figures, not results from a real support team.

Claude Opus 5.5 won 27 of 28 matchups on the incident brief, leading at 1,757.3 Elo. Here the challenge is restraint: 1,200 failed requests are not 1,200 affected people, suspected duplicate charges are not confirmed duplicates, and an unverified note demanding an assurance of safety is not an instruction to obey.

Those results suggest useful candidates for further testing on analysis and synthesis work. They do not establish that the winning answers meet every requirement: Jev's preferences still need separate validation.

4. An unbeaten answer is still not a verified answer

There were two clean sweeps: MiMo-V2.6-Flash went 28-0 on writing, and GLM 5.3 Prime went 28-0 on the mathematics question. Those are strong preferences within this field of answers. They are not independent checks of the recommendations or derivations.

The distinction is especially important for programming. The prompt requests executable code and tests, but this benchmark does not run that code. A judge can prefer an explanation that contains a bug. Before adopting a solution, we would want the tests executed and the edge cases checked.

The cheaper answers were worth a closer look

The cost column adds another useful dimension. OpenRouter reported $0.01715819 for MiMo-V2.6-Flash's six saved answers, compared with $1.35776 for Claude Fable 5.1's six saved answers. Flash placed first overall; Fable placed second. For these saved responses, Fable's total was about 79 times higher.

That is enough to make Flash worth testing on a relevant workload. It is not enough to call it 79 times better value in production. These totals depend on the prompts, generated output, reasoning usage, and provider pricing for this run. They exclude failed or retried attempts and Jev's judging charges. They are not the experiment's full bill.

The explorer shows a weighted mean cost per answer beside the overall ranking, rather than the six-answer sum used above. With six equally weighted questions, that is the total divided by six.

Value v1: weigh Elo against cost

A model can rank highly and still be too expensive for your workload. We added Value v1 to make that tradeoff explicit: a score on a 0-100 scale combining 70% quality and 30% affordability. Here, "quality" means transformed Jev-preference Elo, not independently verified correctness. Higher Elo raises the score; higher answer cost lowers it.

We first put Elo and cost on comparable scales, then combine them:

  • Quality component: Q = 1 / (1 + 10^((1500 - Elo) / 400)). At 1,500 Elo, Q is 0.5.
  • Affordability component: A = 1 / (1 + cost / 0.01). Cost is the weighted mean USD per saved answer. At $0.01, A is 0.5; at a reported $0, it is 1.
  • Combined score: Value v1 = 100 × (0.7 × Q + 0.3 × A). A model at both reference points scores 50.

Use the score as a shortlist, not a verdict

  • If answer quality matters most: start with Elo for the task closest to yours, then inspect and validate the answers. A cost saving is irrelevant if the output fails your requirements.
  • If you have a strict budget: compare cost among models whose answers meet your needs. The overall average can hide a more expensive task.
  • If you want our default balance: sort the live explorer by Value v1 and inspect its cost-versus-Elo plot. Both original inputs remain visible.
  • If you prefer different weights: download the JSON data and recalculate 100 × (w × Q + (1 - w) × A), choosing w between 0 and 1. For example, w = 0.9 gives quality 90% and affordability 10%. The published Value v1 stays fixed at 70/30; this is your own alternative, not a setting in the explorer.

Only complete results with known saved-answer costs receive a score; missing cost is not treated as free. Reported free-tier prices may be temporary. Costs still exclude retries and judge charges, and the score does not measure latency, reliability, accuracy, or return on investment. The weights and reference points are explicit choices, not a universal definition of value. The methodology documents them so you can reproduce or replace the calculation.

Generation settings were not identical budgets across models either. The app records the requested output allowance on each answer, and some truncated attempts needed a higher allowance before producing a complete answer. The two new prompts requested low reasoning with an initial 8,192-token cap; Mistral maps that to its supported minimal mode, recorded as effort none. Explicit truncation retries can exceed the initial cap. Other sampling parameters use provider defaults. We therefore describe these as the results of our configured workflow, not a controlled equal-compute comparison.

How to read the Elo rankings without overreading them

Elo turns a sequence of head-to-head wins and losses into a relative rating. Here, each model starts each question at 1,500. Every saved match is replayed once with K = 32, which controls the size of rating updates. The overall score is the weighted arithmetic mean of completed per-question ratings.

There are a few consequences worth keeping next to the leaderboard:

  • Elo is not an accuracy percentage. A score of 1,700 does not mean an answer is 17% better than one rated 1,450. These ratings are relative to this set of opponents.
  • Replay order matters. We use a stable hashed model-ID order so the same stored dataset produces the same result. That is reproducibility, not order independence. New opponents can change existing ratings without changing any old judgment.
  • One judge is one perspective. Jev is asked to choose a winner even when answers are close; there is no tie option. We have not compared its choices with a human panel here.
  • Blinding has limits. Model labels are omitted, but answer style or self-identification can still reveal origin. We do not repeat each pair with A/B positions swapped.
  • Confidence is not correctness. Jev's confidence describes its decision distribution. It does not establish the probability that a proof, program, or piece of advice is right.

And the 2,436 comparisons are not 2,436 independent examples of model ability. They reuse just 174 saved answers across six prompts. More pairings make the tournament complete; they do not make the task sample broad.

The saved judgments also do not contain a written explanation of why Jev picked a winner. We can inspect which answer it selected and how its probabilities were distributed, but we cannot turn that into a claim that it preferred a particular sentence, tone, or algorithm. To investigate those causes, we would need a separate experiment that changes one feature at a time.

For a practical comparison, choose a task that resembles your workload, read the top answers, and check what they missed against the rubric. Then test the promising models on your own examples. A complete tournament is useful evidence; relevance to your actual job still has to be established.

What we would test next

The next useful step is more independent prompts, not more precision after the decimal point. We would also repeat generations, swap answer positions, run the programming tests, check the mathematical solutions, and ask humans to review a sample of disagreements. Those checks are future work, not features we claim to have completed.

For now, the benchmark gives us something concrete to inspect: the same questions answered by many models, with every head-to-head decision available. Start with the personal-advice results if you want to see where the overall ordering stops being a useful shortcut.
